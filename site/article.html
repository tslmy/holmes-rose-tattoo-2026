Four months of trying to remaster a 1996 Sherlock Holmes adventure with AI: what an image model will and will not keep, why a detective game is harder than a screenshot, and how 65 of 80 rooms got redrawn without moving a single clue.

The boxed figures are live. Drag across a picture to compare it with the 1996 original, and click the buttons above it to swap in another attempt. Numbered notes open on tap. Every number comes from the project's working documents, which are on this site too (All pages, top left).

The Lost Files of Sherlock Holmes: The Case of the Rose Tattoo came out in 1996. Its 80 rooms are pre-rendered 3D scenes at 640×480 in 256 colours, with hand-placed characters walking through them. This project runs the game on a modified ScummVM that can load replacement art from a folder, and tries to fill that folder with something better.The engine work lives in a fork of ScummVM. The override contract is an environment variable, SCUMMVM_SHERLOCK_TATTOO_ASSET_OVERRIDES, that points the engine at a folder of @2x PNGs keyed by scene, resource and frame.

“Something better” turned out to be the hard part. This page walks through every method tried so far, roughly in order, with the art each one produced.

1. Why a detective game is hard to redraw

The first idea anyone has is to hand each screenshot to an image model and ask for a photograph. The trouble is that this is an adventure game. A door has to stay where the door is, because the player clicks on it. A sign has to say what it says, because it is a clue. If the model helpfully adds a second door, Holmes looks better while solving a different case.

Worse, a room is not one picture. What you see on screen is assembled at run time:

LayerWhat it isHow many
BackgroundThe empty room, one image per scene (some scroll)80
Room objectsChairs, busts, lamps, people standing still; drawn as sprites, some animated32,697 frames
Cutscene animations (cAnims)Holmes picking something up, a door opening; each frame copies part of the room behind the figure767 animations, 33,189 frames
Walk spritesHolmes, Watson and the cast, re-projected into each room's 256-colour palette18,380 palette-qualified frames

Clicks are tested against rectangles and walk zones stored in the room data, in the original 640×480 coordinates.From the plan: TattooScene::findBgShape hit-tests with getNewBounds().contains(pt). Gameplay geometry depends on each frame's canvas size and offset, not on its alpha. So a redrawn background must keep its furniture where the game thinks it is, the sprites drawn over it must match its new look, and nothing the story depends on may appear, vanish or change its words.

2. Three bursts of work

The repository has 249 commits, in three clusters. Click one to see what it was.

3. August: ask a diffusion model nicely

The look the project is after came from a conversation with ChatGPT: each scene re-rendered as a photograph of a physical set, with real materials, practical lighting and real-looking people. Those reference images also recompose freely: the camera moves, furniture shifts, figures appear. That is fine for a picture and fatal for a playable room.

The August work tried to get that look while keeping the room. Each method below ran on the same scenes; scene 4, the police station, is the hardest, because its sign is a clue the player must read.

Every model told the same story. Once the strength is high enough to look different, the model drifts the geometry, moves or invents objects, and rewrites signs. Turn it down until text survives (FLUX at --steps 12 --strength 0.25, three real denoising steps) and the result looks like the original.A FLUX run with a hard “geometry lock” pasted the original's edges back over the redraw. With the lock on, the seams showed. The lock masks are among the kept samples; pack-archive lists them. A particularly convincing hallucination is still a hallucination.

Two more routes were set up and never finished. A Z-Image Turbo spot test worked at low strength, but compositing the original mask back over it damaged the picture. A paid GPT Image pass with protect masks was scripted and never run through.Its folder holds one scene's edit mask and nothing else. The script is described in code-archive, with the commit that still has it.

4. August: lifelike characters

Rooms were only half the problem. Holmes and Watson are sprites about 180 pixels tall, animated frame by frame. The August idea was to generate one photoreal character sheet per character, ten poses on a green screen, and composite the nearest pose onto each walk frame.

The single frames look more like a person. The animation does not. A walk cycle has dozens of distinct poses and a ten-pose sheet has ten, so the nearest pose is often wrong: frame 24 walks to the right, and its lifelike replacement turns its back. Poses snap from frame to frame, and the face changes with them. A generic “Victorian detective” prompt lost who the character was, so each character got its own profile, which helped faces and not motion.

The five August playable packs (v2 to v6) put the cinematic rooms and these sprites together. They proved the engine side worked, the override folder, the hi-res compositor, the text renderer, and showed a second problem: photographic rooms with sprite-art people look worse than either alone.

5. October: enlarge, don't redraw

The October work went the other way. Instead of asking a model to redraw a room from a prompt, it used Real-ESRGAN, a super-resolution model, to enlarge every asset 2× from its own pixels. Nothing gets invented at the scale of a door or a sign, and nothing moves.The ESRGAN benchmark on this Mac: 20 sprite frames in 4.24 s on Apple's GPU, 1.6 GB peak. The untiled 1280×960 map ran out of memory; overlapping tiles worked.

The hard part was the sprites. Each one has a hard-edged 1-bit mask in 256 colours; enlarged, the edges either stair-step or smear. A ladder of small pilot packs, all on scene 26, the billiard academy, settled it one layer at a time:

The result was the contour candidate: ESRGAN backgrounds plus five audited sprite trees, 3,640 resources and 87,656 frames. In a smoke test, all 80 scenes reached their start marker. It was faithful and clean, and it was the same game, only larger: dither patterns scaled up with everything else. It became the baseline that every later method has to beat, and the fallback for every room that fails.

6. November: a plan built around the failures

In November the work moved to Claude, starting with a written plan. Its central idea answers the August failure directly: let the model move things, and move the game's geometry with it.

The game keeps running in its original 640×480 coordinates. Each redrawn room ships a smooth, invertible warp from the original coordinates to the new picture, fitted from feature matches, optical flow and the room's own named objects found again in the redraw. Sprites are drawn through the warp, and the mouse is mapped back through its inverse before any hit test. What a warp cannot express (objects swapping order, appearing, vanishing, or a camera move that folds the mapping) fails a gate. Until the engine side exists (Phase 4), assemble runs the warp the other way, pulling each redraw back onto the 1996 layout.The warp is m(p) = s·(p + d(p)): a global scale times a smooth displacement on a 40 px grid, fitted from SIFT + MAGSAC, DIS optical flow and OWLv2 object anchors.

The other decisions follow from August too: de-dither before anything else, since both super-resolution and diffusion amplify the dither; redraw a plate (the background with its static objects composited in) once so lighting matches, then cut the objects back out; cast each character once and redraw every frame conditioned on the original pose; and never wait for a human. Anything that needs approval goes into a review queue with a provisional default, and work carries on.

Phase 0 (14 November) built that skeleton: the CLI and its config, a local LLM client, the queue runner and review queue, an index of the old pilot trees, and a golden set of seven scenes chosen to cover a street, an interior, a haze room, a clue with writing, a scrolling room and a dark room, plus Holmes and Watson.

7. Phase 1: the bake-off

Phase 1 ran every candidate backend on the golden rooms at three freedom levels: L0 and L1 are image-to-image at strength 0.6 and 0.45, with the layout locked; L2 is a free reference edit, with the fitted warp absorbing whatever moves.

Two results shaped everything after. First, a no-op can win. FLUX.3 at L2 returned the input almost unchanged, so it passed every geometry gate and scored highest; the vision model rated the original 3D renders about as photoreal as a real remaster. A minimum-change gate (mean change of at least 20 of 255) fixed that. Second, image-to-image cannot keep a layout. At strength 0.6 the billiard table grows and the door moves; at 0.45 a second barman appears. L0 and L1 were dropped. FLUX.2 klein 9B at L2 became the backend, on the image server, at about 63 seconds a picture.Run locally through mflux on this Mac, the same model took 144 s and 23.2 GB peak per image, which is why diffusion moved to the second laptop.

Phase 1 ended with 2 of 7 rooms passing. Then the human looked. In scene 4 the model had put flames behind the police-station windows; in scene 9 it had added table lamps and lit the fire. No gate asked about new light. Phase 1b added light and people checks and re-ran 56 redraws: 0 of 7. The model fills empty floor with people on its own (a woman, a man and a dog in scene 2 at every seed), and neither a “keep the figures” instruction nor masking the people out stopped it. Phase 1c relaxed the rule to what matters for the story (new lamps are fine; fire outside a fireplace is not) and tried a ControlNet route: 1 of 7, scene 9, with ControlNet at 0 of 7 and worse than the original.

The sprite test came out better. With a cast sheet passed as a second reference image, both characters passed flicker, identity and pose gates on the 4B model, at 8.5 to 13 seconds a frame. The detail of coat folds and scarf stripes still shifted from frame to frame, which is where Phase 3 starts.

9. What failed, and why

Most of the time went into failures. The useful ones, in the order they were met:

Strength is not a safety dial

August. Low enough to keep text means too low to change the look. Every model, every setting. The answer was gates that check objects and writing, and a warp that tolerates movement, rather than trusting the model to stay still.

A pose sheet is not an animation

August. Ten poses cannot stand in for each frame of a walk; poses snap and identity drifts. Phase 3 redraws every frame, conditioned on the original frame and a cast sheet.

The best-scoring redraw changed nothing

Phase 1. FLUX.3 returned its input and topped every score. Hence a minimum-change gate.

The model adds people

Phases 1-2, the most common failure. Instructions and masking did not stop it; detecting the added people and painting them out did (iteration 3). Three rooms still fail the vision check after eight seeds and three repairs.

The gate misjudged the original

Iteration 7. In nine baseline rooms the object detector cannot find a lamp or a mail slot in the 1996 room itself, so it could never find it in a redraw. Rule since then: the original must pass the gate it is judged by, and newly passing rooms are checked by eye.

Not every bug is the model's

Iteration 8. Room-object overrides had sat in the wrong folder since iteration 2, so the 1996 railing, bushes and wall cover drew over the redraws. Several rooms that looked like model failures were pack bugs. Likewise, three “engine crashes” in the play-test came from the test's own scene jump.

10. Who did the work

The November plan split the labour. Claude (Opus) wrote specs, interfaces and acceptance tests, reviewed diffs and gate results, and designed the C++ engine changes. Implementation went, where it could, to OpenCode running on a local model, qwen3.8-27b on the second laptop, as queued delegate jobs with a spec, a list of files it may change and a test. The same local model wrote every scene brief and judged every vision QA question. Diffusion ran on the second laptop, too.In Phase 1 about 10% of engineering lines were delegated, plus all briefs and QA judgement; Phase 2 started delegation-first. When the model host went down mid-task in iteration 5, Opus wrote that piece itself and logged why.

The human set the art direction, answered approval questions when convenient, and played the game. Nothing waited for those answers: every decision has a provisional default in the review queue, and an overturned one re-queues exactly the assets it touched.

11. What is next

  • Phase 3, sprites (started). Characters are still the October upscales, so photoreal rooms carry sprite-art people. The plan: research sprite and pose-conditioned models, cast the main characters, redraw walk cycles frame by frame from keyframes plus flow propagation, with a temporal warp error as the gate, then the long tail of cAnims and NPCs, graded per room.
  • Phase 4, engine (a third done). The room warp for drawing and mouse input, haze effects in true colour so the five haze rooms can be redrawn, palette fades on true-colour art, mixed 2×/4× assets, and the play-test crashes in rooms 33, 36 and 57.
  • Phase 5, animation. Smoother motion: interpolate positions in the renderer, then offline in-between frames.
  • Phase 6, validation and release. A natural playthrough from the start of the game, a short reproduction guide, and this write-up.

The working documents behind every number here are on this site. Good places to start: the bake-off and iteration results, the index of old pilot trees, the pack archive and the code archive.
